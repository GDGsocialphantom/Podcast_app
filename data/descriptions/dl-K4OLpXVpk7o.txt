What does a city gain when it stops asking "Who, us?" And what does it cost if it loses that confidence?

On this episode of The Disruption Lab, recorded live during Global Entrepreneurship Week, host Kevin McGinnis sits down with Neil Sharma, founder of DEG and vice chair of the KC2026 World Cup Host Committee.

Sharma starts in 2000, when Kansas City had no venture studios, no organized entrepreneurial ecosystem, and an investor class that made its money in real estate, commodities, and logistics and couldn't see why a tech startup belonged in a portfolio. He walks through how DEG grew from a two-person web shop into a 650-person global firm acquired by Dentsu, and why the turning point wasn't funding. It was signing Hallmark, then telling every prospect in the country, "Hallmark trusts us, so you can trust us too." That story anchors his framework of the three forms of capital: financial capital matters least, intellectual capital matters more, and relationship capital matters most.

From there, the conversation moves to KC2026. Sharma explains why the World Cup's lasting return is brand and confidence rather than visitor spending: Kansas City led every host city in Nielsen viewership share, hosted base camps for four national teams, and drew global press coverage most locals never saw. He describes the city's shift from an inferiority complex to what he calls intrinsic confidence, and why that shift has economic consequences.

The back half looks ahead: Kansas City's lead in sports architecture, a regional pipeline of major venue projects, the World Economic Forum's projection that sports will become a multi-trillion-dollar industry, why sports media is the region's most underrated asset (and why ESPN being in Bristol, Connecticut was an accident of history), how sports innovation feeds health and bioscience, and why the Missouri–Kansas state line remains the biggest obstacle to becoming a global sports tech hub.

Sharma closes with his definition of what makes Kansas City different: a positive-sum economy of makers, a pay-it-forward culture where "it's how fast you make a seat for the next person," and a city becoming global without losing the habit of knowing each other.
