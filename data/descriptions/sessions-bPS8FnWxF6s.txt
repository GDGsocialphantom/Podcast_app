What actually happens when people who disagree on everything are forced to share the same moment?

On this continued Keystone Session, speaker and author Lauren Schiffer continues with a scene from a city park: two men locked in a heated, nearly-physical argument about politics until a toddler chasing a ball trips and falls. Instantly, both men are on their knees checking on her, waving down her mother, forgetting the fight entirely. Schiffer uses the moment to make her case for embracing diversity: not by ignoring what makes people different, but by starting from what nearly all of us already share. She puts the number at 99 percent, the same foods that nourish us, the same losses that grieve us, the same need to belong to something bigger than ourselves.

From there, she builds out the practical architecture for living that idea: a three-question test for picking which battles are actually worth fighting (is it fundamental to your safety, your serenity, or your productivity?), the discipline behind maintaining a working attitude on the days it doesn't come naturally, and a story about her father's failed remote-control airplane that, decades later, led to the composite materials that make the B-2 bomber invisible to radar, which is proof, she argues, that today's stumbling block is often tomorrow's stepping stone.
