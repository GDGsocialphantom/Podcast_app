Where does Kansas's water actually come from, and why is it getting harder to reach?

On part 2 of 3 of this live Keystone Session, our representative of the Kansas Geological Survey takes us underground into the two aquifer systems that supply most of the state's water, and the field work it takes to monitor them. That work includes snow drifts, landowners, and a frozen possum blocking a well. The first system is the Kansas River alluvial aquifer, a fast-reacting system that moves almost in lockstep with the river above it. The second is the High Plains Aquifer, the largest groundwater supply in Kansas and one of the largest in the country, stretching from South Dakota to Texas. It took millions of years to fill. In parts of western Kansas, the water table now sits 400 feet down and drops a little further every year.

The most uncomfortable finding may be the simplest one: the biggest threat to Kansas's groundwater isn't Oklahoma or anyone else downstream. It's what Kansas does with the water itself.
