Why are energy companies from the U.S., Australia and the U.K. drilling thousands of feet into Kansas granite, looking for a fuel that may not flow on its own?

On part 3 of 3 of this live Keystone Session, Brendan Bream, a geologist with the Kansas Geological Survey, explains the science and the economics behind one of the strangest energy plays in the country: natural hydrogen. About a billion years ago, North America tried to split apart. That failed rift left a belt of iron-rich rock running under Kansas. When water moving east from the Rocky Mountains reacts with those rocks, it can release hydrogen gas. That gas collects along a buried feature called the Nemaha Ridge, where nearly every exploration company is now focused.

Bream is candid about where things stand. The original hope was simple: drill a well, tap a gas cap, and let the hydrogen flow. That doesn’t look likely to work. The next idea is stimulated hydrogen, which means injecting fluids to trigger the reaction underground. Nobody yet knows the reaction rates or which fluids work best.

So why come to Kansas at all? Bream points to a century of oil and gas experience, a well that costs about a million dollars and can be permitted in a couple of months, idle drilling rigs, and what he calls the best public well dataset in the country.
