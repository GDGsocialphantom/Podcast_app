What happens to a region whose entire economy runs on water that won't come back for 300 years?

On part 3 of 3 of our Keystone Sessions talk, our representative of the Kansas Geological Survey lays out the reality of the High Plains (Ogallala) Aquifer in western Kansas. In some counties, 60 to 70 percent of the aquifer is already gone. Recharge from rainfall is about half an inch a year, so the region is effectively mining its groundwater. The driver isn't drought. It's pumping: in some counties, irrigation accounts for 97 percent of all water use.

The talk walks through the Survey's "estimated usable lifetime" map, which shows some areas with roughly 25 years left before wells can't sustain a center pivot. It then weighs the fixes people propose, from a Missouri River aqueduct to railing water west to treating oilfield produced water. Finally, it turns to the one approach already working: farmers in Wichita and Sheridan counties who voluntarily cut their pumping and stabilized their water levels.
