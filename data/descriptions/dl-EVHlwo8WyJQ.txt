Who builds the art you walk past every day? And what happens to a city when nobody local can?

On this episode of The Disruption Lab, host Kevin McGinnis sits down with Dick Jobe, founder of Machine Head, an art metal fabrication shop in Kansas City’s East Crossroads. For 25 years Dick was “Dr. Volvo,” one of the most trusted mechanics in the city. At the top of that trade, he sold part of his car collection, bought machine tools, went back to school for welding and metalwork, and started over. His first clients were artists from the Crossroads galleries next door.

Today Machine Head’s work includes an 85-foot bird-wing skeleton suspended over baggage claim in Terminal C at John Wayne Airport in Orange County, built with artist Beth Nybeck; a faceted, LED-lit “silver nugget” DJ booth for the W Hotel in Aspen; Gatorade Victory Lane at Kansas Speedway and Homestead-Miami Speedway; and a 6,000-pound climbable bicycle for kids in Roeland Park. Most of it carries an artist’s name, not his.

Underneath the stories is a systems argument. Public art depends on a layer of fabricators most people never see. When that capacity doesn’t exist locally, the commissions go elsewhere, the money leaves, and the skill never develops. Dick explains how the Kansas City Art Institute has fed that layer for decades, how property tax protections kept his shop in the Crossroads when rising costs pushed other makers out, and how he’s handing 25 years of knowledge to the next generation before he steps back.
