Every year, Kansas can pump about seven million acre-feet of water for irrigation. That's roughly 70% of all the water the state uses, and much of it comes from underground.

On part 1 of 3 of this live Keystone Session, a presenter from the Kansas Geological Survey explains how groundwater works and why it matters across the western U.S. The first part of the story is geography. Because of the Rocky Mountain rain shadow, rainfall drops from nearly 40 inches a year in Kansas City to about 18 at the Colorado line. That shift turns at the 100th meridian, near Dodge City, where the state goes from a surface-water economy in the east to one that runs almost entirely on aquifers in the west. Some western counties have no reservoirs and no streams. Every drop they use comes out of the ground.

The talk covers who uses that water: cities, power plants, wildlife refuges, irrigation, and a livestock industry with twice as many cattle as there are people in the state. It also explains the prior appropriation rule that decides who gets water when supplies run short. Under that rule, the oldest permit wins, no matter what the water is used for.

Then comes the hard part: measuring something no one can see. That includes decades of drillers' logs, a helicopter towing a 60-foot antenna to create a CAT-scan-style map of the High Plains Aquifer, and water levels still checked by hand with a 500-foot steel tape coated in blue chalk.
