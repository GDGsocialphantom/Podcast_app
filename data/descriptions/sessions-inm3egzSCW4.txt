What does it take to build a bridge column that can survive an earthquake, stay standing through catastrophic damage, and then get repaired in a single afternoon?

Mohammed Abdel-Aziz is an assistant professor of structural engineering and director of the Structural Engineering Lab at the University of Missouri-Kansas City. A PhD graduate of Missouri University of Science and Technology, his research focuses on resilient and sustainable structures: steel, concrete, and fiber-reinforced polymer composites tested at full scale inside one of the region's largest structural testing facilities.

The starting point is a national problem: of the 622,000 bridges in the U.S., 43% need rehabilitation and 35% need full replacement. One in three bridges are at risk, driven by flooding, scouring, collision, overload, fire, and seismic events. Missouri alone has more than 24,600 bridges, the seventh-most of any state, and 52% have outlived their intended service life. Hundreds still use steel pile columns built during World War II, when there wasn't time to cure concrete and the corrosion loss those bridges have already sustained exceeds what current design standards project for the next hundred years.

Abdel-Aziz walks through two innovations built and tested inside the SEL. The first is Accelerated Bridge Construction: prefabricated columns, beams, and footings built off-site and assembled on-site like Lego pieces, cutting cost and construction time without sacrificing quality. The second is a new column design for seismically active regions near the New Madrid fault: a hollow steel tube wrapped in FRP with concrete sandwiched between, and no conventional rebar at all. Tested to failure, it reached four times the drift of a conventional column before yielding, while staying standing. Even after severe damage Abdel-Aziz's team restored the column's strength with a rapid FRP repair in six hours. A separate technique applies the same composite material as a "Band-Aid," encasing corroded steel sections in a carbon-fiber tube and filling it with concrete to restore a damaged bridge's load path without full replacement.
