Every year, an average of 14,600 vehicles collide with bridges across the U.S.: trucks clipping girders, overheight loads tearing into overpasses. When it happens on U.S. Highway 60 in Springfield, Missouri, the damage cascaded from the first girder to the second to the third, and the highway closed. The standard answer is to replace the bridge. Mohammed Abdel-Aziz's answer is: not necessarily.

This is part 2 of Abdel-Aziz's Session here at Keystone. As the director of the Structural Engineering Lab at the University of Missouri-Kansas City, his team is building and full-scale testing repair strategies for two of the most common ways bridges fail: corrosion and impact, before those repairs ever reach a real structure.

The first is corrosion repair. Steel bridge piles that have lost significant thickness underwater get wrapped in a fiber-reinforced polymer tube filled with concrete, essentially a structural Band-Aid. Abdel-Aziz's team validates the design computationally first, using Ansys LS-DYNA simulation, then proves it physically under full axial load in the SEL. The result: no measurable loss in the repaired section, with all damage pushed safely outside the repair zone. Some of these repairs use ultra-high-performance concrete (UHPC) reinforced with steel, basalt, or glass-fiber microfibers, designed to bridge cracks and reinforce the one thing plain concrete is bad at: tension.

The second is impact repair. Abdel-Aziz's lab runs full-scale crash tests on AASHTO Type II bridge girders to classify real-world impact damage and design a fiber-reinforced polymer or UHPC repair that restores a girder's strength without full replacement. The tests run for seconds; each one takes months of computational modeling to set up correctly. The same modeling approach also opens the door to real-time structural health monitoring, predicting how much strength a damaged column or girder has actually lost, so a repair can be sized to the real damage instead of over-engineered for a worst case that isn't there.

None of this happens in isolation. Abdel-Aziz frames the SEL as a hub connecting research, industry, and education with partners including Geiger, Western Form, and Ferroglobe in Kansas City, and material and research partners reaching Penn State, Michigan, Florida, Texas, and Virginia. Graduate and undergraduate students test alongside him, present at ACI and ASCE conferences, and take AISC-sponsored field trips, training that's meant to close the gap between classroom theory and what actually holds up a bridge.
